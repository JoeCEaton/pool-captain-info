I need twelve testers

Pool Captain is finished enough to use in a real match, and now it has to get through Google's closed-testing requirement before it can go on the Play Store.

The short version

Google requires 12 testers who stay opted in for 14 consecutive days before an app from a new personal developer account can be published. That's the whole reason this page exists. You install it, use it on league night, and tell me what broke or annoyed you.

What you need

  • An Android phone, and the Google account email you use on it — that address is what gets added to the tester list. (An iPhone version is planned; it isn't ready to test yet.)
  • About two minutes to install it, and then whatever attention you feel like giving it over two weeks.
  • Ideally you play APA 8-ball or 9-ball, or you use Fargo ratings — that's who the app is for. Testers who don't play league are still useful for finding things that are simply confusing.

What testing actually involves

  1. Send me your Google account email. I add it to the tester list — nothing appears until I do, so this step is unavoidable.
  2. Accept the invite and install. You'll get a Play Store link that installs the app like any normal download. It updates itself the same way too.
  3. Use it. Log a couple of matches, measure a few breaks, rate your home table. Poking at it for ten minutes is genuinely enough to be useful.
  4. Stay opted in for 14 days. This is the part Google actually counts. Please don't uninstall inside the two weeks — if you do, the clock resets for everybody.
  5. Tell me what you found. A one-line text is fine. Crashes, numbers that look wrong, wording that made no sense, a break it refused to hear — all of it helps.

What I'd most like feedback on

  • Does the break measurement hear your break in a real, noisy poolroom?
  • Do the skill-level readings from your logged matches match your gut sense of how you're playing?
  • Is anything slow, ugly, or baffling on your particular phone?

Analysis

APA 8 Ball — what this matchup is actually worth

Set both skill levels and the app fills in everything else: the Fargo range that level covers, and the race each player is on from the Equalizer chart. Then it counts every way the match can end.

  • Single-game odds from the gap between the two Fargo ratings.
  • Every possible score line, each with its own probability — not just a win percentage, but how likely 3–0 is against 2–1.
  • APA match points for each score line, on the three-point scale, so you can see which results are worth playing for rather than only which are likely.
  • Overall match odds and expected match points for both players.
  • Skill level steppers set the race automatically, and you can override the Fargo rating or the race if you know better.

Useful for captains deciding a lineup, and for working out whether a mismatch on paper is really as lopsided as it looks.

Fargo Match — for races that aren't APA races

The same odds engine, driven straight off two Fargo ratings, with race lengths you set yourself — anything up to a race to 50. Built for tournament brackets, gambling sets, and any league that handicaps on Fargo instead of APA skill levels.

  • Single-game and whole-match odds for any pair of ratings and any two race lengths.
  • A games handicap in the BCA style, so you can propose a fair spot instead of arguing about one.
  • Points to tie on a 17-point scale, for formats scored that way.
Analysis + Log

Break Speed — your phone as a radar gun

You don't need a radar gun to know how hard you break. You need the distance the cue ball travelled and the time it took. Pool Captain gets the distance from the table and the time from the two cracks your break makes: the tip hitting the cue ball, and the cue ball hitting the rack.

Setting up the distance

  • Table presets from a 6-or-7-foot bar box up to a 12-foot table.
  • Drag the cue ball anywhere behind the head string — it won't let you place it illegally — and the travel distance to the rack updates as you move. Move it to the rail for a cut break.
  • Or just type a distance, in inches or centimetres.

Timing the break

  • Put the phone on the rail, hit Record a break, and break normally. It reads the room's noise floor first so a loud bar doesn't fool it, then waits up to 25 seconds while you line up.
  • It shows you the waveform it heard with a marker on each crack. If it put one in the wrong place, drag it — zoom in with + and −, and the interval and speed update as you drag.
  • A confidence warning appears when it isn't sure it found the cue strike, rather than quietly reporting a wrong number.
  • Five sensitivity steps, and when it hears nothing usable it tells you the loudest sound it did hear against the level it needed — so you know whether to break closer or turn it up.
  • Result in mph or km/h, with an honest note that this is average speed across the break, a few mph under what a radar gun reads off the tip.
Log

Break Log — is your break getting better?

Every measured break can be saved, and the log turns them into a trend instead of a number you forget by next Tuesday.

  • Best, average, and total across everything you've logged.
  • A speed-over-time chart with your best and average marked.
  • Per-break rows showing the date, the speed, the distance and interval behind it, which table it was measured on, and a confidence dot so a shaky reading is visible as one.
  • Delete any break, with an undo if you didn't mean it.
Analysis

Table — how tough is the table you're on?

A 0.90 bar box and a 1.20 tournament table are not the same game, and your percentages shouldn't be compared as if they were. This rates the table in front of you using Dr. Dave Alciatore's Table Difficulty Factor, from four measurements you can take with a tape measure.

  • An annotated pocket photo showing exactly what mouth, throat, and shelf mean, because those are the measurements people get wrong.
  • Pick your table size, mouth width, throat, and shelf depth from ranges rather than needing exact figures.
  • Get a TDF number and a verdict — Too Easy through Too Tough — plus a plain sentence like "about 12% tougher than a standard table".
  • Save each room you play in by name. The difficulty bar then shows your easiest and hardest tables, your average, and where the table you're rating now falls between them.
Log

APA 9 Ball Logging — the level your pace really says

Your handicap is what the league assigned you. Your scoring pace is what you're actually doing. Log your matches and the app works out both, so you can see a bump coming instead of being surprised by it.

  • Log a match in a few taps: date, skill level, innings, defensive shots, points scored, match points, win or loss.
  • It computes your points per inning over the best 10 of your last 20 — the same window the league's own scoring logic leans on — and turns that into the skill level you're performing at, with how far through that level you are.
  • A red ▲ or blue ▼ says how far above or below your assigned level you're playing, in levels rather than jargon.
  • A chart of every match with the skill-level bands shaded behind it, so a good night and a bad night are visible at a glance. Switch it between pace, adjusted innings, points, match points, and win rate.
  • The match table flags which matches are counting (ranked 1–10) and which one is about to age out of your window.
  • A Gate Floor toggle shows what your numbers look like under the floor the league applies to a player at the level below.

Compare the whole team on one axis

Once you're logging more than one player — a whole roster, if you're a captain — the comparison view ranks everybody against the pace expected at their own level.

  • Every player's bar sits against their own skill band, so an SL3 and an SL7 can be read side by side without the SL7 automatically looking better.
  • Colour says the story at a glance: pushing up, holding, or soft against their level.
  • Switch between recent form (everything in the last 20) and what they're graded on (the best 10), because those answer different questions.
  • Tap a name to jump straight into that player's match history.
Log

APA 8 Ball Logging — innings per win, done properly

8-ball has no points, so pace means innings. The catch is that innings are shared between both players and the Equalizer gives everyone a different race, which is what makes a raw innings-per-win number so easy to misread. This handles both.

  • Log your skill level and your opponent's — the form shows you the race both players are on, so the Wins field is never ambiguous.
  • Records innings, your defensive shots, racks you won, racks the opponent won, and match points. Opponent racks are optional; leave it blank and the app is honest about what it can't then compute.
  • Reports innings per win, innings per rack played, and your win percentage over the best 10 of your last 20, with the counting matches ranked in the table.
  • A zero-rack match still costs you — its innings count, its wins don't. Losing badly isn't free, and the app doesn't pretend otherwise.
  • The comparison view grades every player against the innings-per-rack range expected at their own level, ranks the room fastest first, and flags anyone far enough ahead of their level to be on bump watch.

The Guide — no number without an explanation

Every tool has a written guide, reachable from the bottom of the screen you're on. Each one covers what the controls do, then how the number is actually worked out — including where a figure is an estimate and where it's a rule straight out of the APA scoresheet.

  • Seven guides, in plain language, with diagrams and worked examples.
  • Honest about its own limits: which values are calibrated estimates, and which parts of the detection can get things wrong.
  • No hunting for it — every screen links to its own guide.

Where your data lives

Short answer: on your phone, and nowhere else.

No account, no server

There's nothing to sign up for. The app makes no network requests of its own and works fully offline — try it in airplane mode. Your match logs, break history, and saved tables sit in the app's private storage on your device.

No ads, no analytics

No advertising, no usage tracking, no crash reporting, no third-party SDKs collecting anything. I don't find out that you used it, let alone how.

The microphone

Used only on the break-speed screen, only while it's listening. Audio is analysed in memory to find the timing of two impacts. No recording is saved or transmitted — what persists is a number.

Your data is portable

Both match logs can be exported as a plain JSON file through the share sheet, and restored on another phone. It's your data, in a format you can read.

Things you should know before you install

It's an early build

That's the point of a test. Expect rough edges. Two known ones: a chart label can clip at the right edge of the break trend, and restoring a backup file into the wrong tracker will fill it with nonsense rather than refusing.

Some numbers are estimates, and the app says so

The APA race charts and 9-ball point requirements are transcribed from APA's own materials. The skill-level bands the comparison screens grade you against are calibrated estimates worked out from match data — they are not published APA figures, and the guide is explicit about which is which.

The screenshots on this page use invented players

Every name and match on this page is fabricated for illustration. The numbers are internally consistent — they were generated to be a plausible logbook and then run through the app's own calculations — but nobody's real record is shown anywhere here.

It isn't affiliated with anyone
